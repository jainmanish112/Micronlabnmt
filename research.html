Research Projects

Antibacterial surfaces for 3D-printed titanium

The NM-INBRE pilot asks whether the surface of a titanium implant can be tuned independently of its bulk alloy. We print Ti and Ti–Cu coupons by laser powder bed fusion, deposit thin metal films, and anneal them to form nanoparticles. Film thickness and annealing conditions let us vary particle size, spacing, and surface coverage.

The study compares Cu, Ag, Au, and Pt films and measures metal release alongside bacterial survival. Osteoblast assays, indentation, and adhesion tests assess how the most promising surfaces affect cells and withstand mechanical loading.

Where fatigue cracks start in metallic multilayers

Mg/Nb and Cu/Nb multilayers provide model systems for studying fatigue at the micro- and nanoscale. Changing the layer thickness can change both slip confinement and, in Mg/Nb, the crystal structure of Mg. We ask which of these changes governs the first irreversible damage under repeated strain.

We compare strain amplitude and cycles to crack initiation with microscopy of slip localization, grain growth, and cracking. The goal is to identify where a strain–life relation describes several architectures and where a change in damage mechanism requires a different description.

Can an oxide interface arrest grain growth during fatigue?

This project pairs physical vapor deposition (PVD) of metal with atomic layer deposition (ALD) of a thin oxide. In model Al/Al₂O₃ and Nb/Nb₂O₅ stacks, we vary metal and oxide thickness independently to test whether the oxide impedes grain-boundary motion during cycling.

We plan to track where damage begins as confinement increases. A shift from grain growth to localized slip or separation at the metal–oxide interface would identify the mechanism that limits fatigue life after grain growth slows.

How ion irradiation changes thin-film fatigue

We compare unirradiated and ion-irradiated Pt and Pt–Au films to ask whether irradiation changes the grain-boundary stability that gives nanocrystalline Pt–Au its fatigue resistance. After exposure, a MEMS resonator subjects the film to repeated strain near 10 kHz.

At matched strain amplitudes, we compare the number of cycles to observable damage and examine grain growth, voids, and crack initiation. Varying ion dose helps distinguish irradiation damage from changes caused by cyclic loading alone.
